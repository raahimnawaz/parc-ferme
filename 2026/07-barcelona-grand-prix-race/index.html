Summary

Three findings

  1. Hamilton's Ferrari had the fastest race pace. With tyre compound and age, fuel burn and traffic taken out, Russell's Mercedes was 0.16 ± 0.07 s a lap slower, Antonelli's Mercedes 0.23 s and Norris's McLaren 0.29 s.

    See pace by sector

  2. Russell's Mercedes was quicker than Hamilton's Ferrari through sector 1 (Turns 1–3, by 0.07 s a lap) but lost 0.18 s a lap in sector 2 (Turns 4–9).

    See pace by sector

  3. Ferrari, Mercedes, McLaren and Red Bull Racing had the most grip in fast corners, 2.8–3.8% above the mean team; Cadillac, Aston Martin and Williams had the least, 3.1–4.1% below. In slow corners Mercedes and Ferrari were ahead, 4.1–4.9% above.

    See grip by team
Car performance

Pace and grip

Every green lap counts: no pit laps, no safety cars, nothing slower than 107% of the car's own median. Laps are compared after taking out what isn't the car: tyre compound and age, fuel burn over the race, and traffic.

Pace is shown car by car, because two drivers of one team can have very different races. Grip is shown team by team, from both cars, because it is a property of the car.

Not measured yet: drag. In 2026 how fast a car accelerates on a straight depends on how much battery energy it deploys there, which the public data doesn't show. Drag will be fitted together with the energy model.

Traffic is taken out, not filtered out. Running within 1 s of the car ahead cost +0.20 ± 0.04 s a lap, +0.15 s of it in sector 2 (Turns 4–9). In sector 1 it was too small to measure. Between 1 and 2 s behind, the cost was +0.02 ± 0.04 s, too small to measure.

Grip in slow and fast corners

From the lowest speed of every green lap through each corner the drivers lift for (13 of 14). Every car takes nearly the same radius through a corner, so comparing a team with the field at the same corner cancels the radius out; the timing data doesn't show where a car is across the track.

The gap between slow and fast corners is where downforce shows. Splitting each team's grip into tyre grip and downforce needs more circuits than one: here the split is uncertain by 1–7% per team, and only the largest differences stand out. Both are in the table.

How far to trust it

Checks

Each model behind the numbers above has to recover known answers on a test bench before it runs on a session, and its output is then checked against what the session's own data can confirm. A session is only published if every gating data check passes.

Models

✓PassGrip · test bench5 synthetic teams with known grip and downforce, run through the lap sim and rendered as OpenF1-style telemetry, then measured like a real session. Largest error 0.31 points on tyre grip (tolerance 0.6) and 1.17 on downforce (tolerance 2).
!WarnGrip · dirty airRefitted on the 615 laps with no car within 1.5 s ahead. Fast-corner grip moved 0.5 points at most (Cadillac). The largest move against its uncertainty: Haas, slow corners, 0.9 points (2.8 standard errors).
✓PassPace · test benchA synthetic race of 6 cars over 60 laps with known sector gaps, tyre, fuel and traffic effects, lap-to-lap noise and a few wrecked laps. Every car's gap in every sector recovered to within 0.026 s (tolerance 0.06 s).
✓PassPace · held-out lapsFitted on odd-numbered laps, asked for the lap times of the 521 even-numbered ones. Median error 0.29 s, against 0.71 s for each car's own median lap.
!WarnField car · corner speedsThe sim's car, with grip and downforce fitted so its corner minimum speeds match the field's (μ 1.45, CLA 4.94 m²). Misses by 12 km/h rms, with a pattern: see below. The team comparisons above don't use it. The modelled line is also 81 m a lap shorter than the cars drove.

Field car against real corner speeds

Simulated minus real minimum speed at each corner the drivers lift for. It is too fast where a corner is the second half of a sequence and at Turn 9, where drivers don't use the full limit, and too slow in the section rebuilt since the track-width survey, where widths are a guess.
